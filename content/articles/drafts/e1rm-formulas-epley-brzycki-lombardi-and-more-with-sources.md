# E1RM Formulas: Epley, Brzycki, Lombardi & More (With Sources)

Your estimated one-rep max (e1RM) is the cornerstone of intelligent strength programming — but the formula you use to calculate it matters more than most people realise. Different formulas were developed by different researchers, validated on different populations, and produce meaningfully different results, especially at higher rep ranges.

This is the complete reference. Every major e1RM formula, with its original source and a plain-English note on when it tends to perform best.

## The Epley Formula

**1RM = Weight × (1 + 0.0333 × Reps)**

The most widely cited e1RM formula, developed by Boyd Epley at the University of Nebraska. Published in the *NSCA Journal* in 1985, it was originally designed for American football players. Epley tends to produce slightly higher estimates than Brzycki, particularly as rep counts climb above 5.

*Source: Epley, B. (1985). Poundage Chart. NSCA Journal, 6(6), 37.*

## The Brzycki Formula

**1RM = Weight ÷ (1.0278 − 0.0278 × Reps)**

Developed by Matt Brzycki and published in the Journal of Physical Education, Recreation & Dance in 1993. Widely regarded as one of the most accurate formulas for sets in the 2–10 rep range. *Source: Brzycki, M. (1993). Strength Testing — Predicting a One-Rep Max from Reps-to-Fatigue. Journal of Physical Education, Recreation & Dance, 64(1), 88–90.*

## The Lombardi Formula

**1RM = Weight × Reps^0.10**

A simpler exponential model developed by V.H. Lombardi. Published in the NSCA Journal in 1989. Lombardi tends to produce higher estimates than Brzycki or Epley and is considered less accurate at higher rep ranges, but is sometimes preferred for its mathematical simplicity.

*Source: Lombardi, V.H. (1989). Beginning Weight Training. WC Brown.*

## The Mayhew Formula

**1RM = (100 × Weight) ÷ (52.2 + 41.9 × e^(−0.055 × Reps))**

Developed by Jerry Mayhew and colleagues, published in the Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research in 1992. Originally validated on college football players using the bench press specifically. Tends to perform well in lower rep ranges (1–6 reps) and is frequently referenced in sports science literature.

*Source: Mayhew, J.L., Ball, T.E., Arnold, M.D., & Bowen, J.C. (1992). Relative muscular endurance performance as a predictor of bench press strength in college men and women. Journal of Applied Sport Science Research, 6(4), 200–206.*

## The O'Conner Formula

**1RM = Weight × (1 + 0.025 × Reps)**

A more conservative formula developed by O'Conner, Simmons & O'Conner in 1989. Produces lower e1RM estimates than Epley or Brzycki, which makes it a useful lower-bound reference — particularly for less experienced lifters where the other formulas may overestimate.

*Source: O'Conner, B., Simmons, J., & O'Conner, P. (1989). Weight training today. West Publishing.*

## The Wathan Formula

**1RM = (100 × Weight) ÷ (48.8 + 53.8 × e^(−0.075 × Reps))**

Developed by David Wathan and published in the NSCA's Essentials of Strength Training and Conditioning in 1994. Similar in structure to Mayhew's formula and tends to produce comparable results. Often used as a cross-validation formula when comparing estimates.

*Source: Wathan, D. (1994). Load assignment. In T.R. Baechle (Ed.), Essentials of Strength Training and Conditioning (pp. 435–446). Human Kinetics.*

## The McGlothin Formula

**1RM = (100 × Weight) ÷ (101.3 − 2.67123 × Reps)**

A linear model developed by McGlothin. Produces estimates that tend to fall between Brzycki and Epley for moderate rep ranges. Less commonly cited in academic literature than the formulas above but included in many professional calculators.

*Source: McGlothin, S. (1984). Maximum based on reps. NSCA Journal, 6(3).*

## Which Formula Should You Use?

There is no single "correct" answer — each formula was validated on a specific population and rep range, and none of them is universally more accurate.

As a general guide: Brzycki and Epley are the most commonly used and perform well for sets of 2–8 reps. For higher rep sets (8+), all formulas become less accurate. Mayhew and Wathan tend to be favoured in sports science research contexts where the bench press is the primary lift being tested.
